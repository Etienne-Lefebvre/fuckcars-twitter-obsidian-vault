## 1/14

2/ Example of adversarial dynamic:  The private consortium (RTG) in charge of the O-train took on 300 million in private financing, of which the city agreed to pay back over 30 years if the project was delivered on time. Large penalties would occur if delivery was late.

*❤ 1 · 🔁 0*

## 2/14

3/ During the recent public hearings, RTG said that this arrangement put them under immense pressure that contributed to rushing the launch and cutting basic safety and maintenance testing.

![[1611485945269723136-Fl0l7RjX0AUnqk8.png]]

*❤ 1 · 🔁 0*

## 3/14

4/ Contract based timelines and financial penalties are common in both P3 and public financing schemes, but when construction delays arise, P3's incentivize finger pointing and an offloading of accountability to other groups, rather than incentivizing a collaborative approach.

*❤ 1 · 🔁 0*

## 4/14

5/ Just look at how Montreal has dealt with delays with the REM project. For those who aren't aware, Montreal rejected the P3 model. Ownership and financing of the whole project was undertaken by public institutions, the main one being Quebec's public pension fund: CDPQ Infra.

*❤ 2 · 🔁 0*

## 5/14

6/ Since CDPQ has full ownership of the project over all development stages, they take on the lion's share of responsibility. This reduces the probability of contract litigation between the construction consortium and the owners/financers of the project.

*❤ 1 · 🔁 0*

## 6/14

7/ When CDPQ observed delays in the first 2 years of construction (labour shortage) they proactively proposed a timeline optimization agreement with the consortium to adjust the construction milestone performance conditions. CDPQ prioritized project quality over contract-payoffs.

*❤ 2 · 🔁 0*

## 7/14

8/ In a P3 setting there's almost no collaborative incentivizes that exist when a project doesn't go according to plan. Rather the risks are understood to be mitigated by a strict interpretation of the contracts and their associated penalties for missing construction milestones.

*❤ 2 · 🔁 0*

## 8/14

9/ Look at the more recent delays with REM's South Shore line. It was scheduled to open in December 2021, it got delayed a year, and now it just got delayed till spring 2023.

*❤ 1 · 🔁 0*

## 9/14

10/ CDPQ knows these delays aren't the fault of the consortium, so CDPQ has no issue taking on the costs associated with delaying the project. CDPQ has a long term incentive in the project, they aren't pressured to meet short term milestones if it means long term consequences.

*❤ 2 · 🔁 0*

## 10/14

11/ This is one of the key failings with Ottawa's O-train. None of the stakeholders had long term incentives in the project.

*❤ 5 · 🔁 0*

## 11/14

12/ Ottawa's mayor pressured the consortium to rush the rollout, to lock-in an easy re-election. And the consortium had an incentive cut corners (to stay within budget) and to quickly hand over ownership to the city to avoid paying for issues that would arise post-rollout.

*❤ 3 · 🔁 0*

## 12/14

13/ It's really hard for me to see how P3 financing isn't the lead culprit with the shit show that happened/is happening in Ottawa.

*❤ 2 · 🔁 0*

## 13/14

14/ Just read statements from CDPQ Execs. Look at this quote for example. It's clear that the top brass of CDPQ are under very little political pressure to prioritize speedy delivery over a high quality product. These prioritizations would be unconventional in a P3 environment.

![[1611485960474185737-Fl0l8IiX0BgQzB2.png]]

*❤ 4 · 🔁 0*

## 14/14

15/ My main point though, is that P3, as a infrastructure financing model, performs poorly because it incentivizes short term over long term considerations, it facilitates political corruption, and it incentivizes competition between stakeholders rather than collaboration.
