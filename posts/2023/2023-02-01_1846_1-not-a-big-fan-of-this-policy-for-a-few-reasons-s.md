## 2/17

2/ To get from A to B, people overwhelmingly choose the most convenient transportation method over the least expensive transportation method. Time is the real cost. If the bus takes you 3 times as long to get from A to B compared to a car, people will choose to drive.

*❤ 347 · 🔁 6*

## 3/17

3/ Considering this, cities should focus on improving the quality of public transit service, meaning better frequency, reliability, speed, ect.. Doing this will cut down on the real costs that people value...time.

*❤ 410 · 🔁 7*

## 4/17

4/ A compounding issue with fare free policies is that it reduces the city's ability to finance expansions to public transit operations + infrastructure.

*❤ 256 · 🔁 0*

## 5/17

5/ Most American cities already have little spending power given the financial burden of maintaining low density suburban sprawl and large urban road and highway systems. So reducing a key revenue source in an already constrained fiscal environment wouldn't be my first choice.

*❤ 181 · 🔁 0*

## 6/17

6/ Fare free policies also disproportionally benefit wealthier suburban residents who take longer trips compared to urban residents who take shorter trips (regressive). A better system would be to have zone based fares and social fares.

*❤ 159 · 🔁 3*

## 7/17

7/ If the concern is about equity it's best to offer cheaper bus passes for residents in X zone or in Y income group, this way you end up creating an equitable/progressive fare system while also being able to generate revenue that the city can re-invest to improve quality.

*❤ 140 · 🔁 1*

## 8/17

8/ I think fare free transit makes most sense if applied strategically rather than universally.

## 9/17

9/ For example, implementing a fare free time-period during off peak hours would make more sense if you are concerned with increasing ridership. Or simply targeting specific low ridership routes and making them fare free.

## 10/17
